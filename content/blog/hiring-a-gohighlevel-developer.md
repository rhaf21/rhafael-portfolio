# How to hire a GoHighLevel developer: six questions to ask first

Hire for the system, not the clicking. A good GoHighLevel developer builds in your account under their own login, tests every workflow with test contacts before it goes live, gives every follow-up a way to stop, quotes a fixed price after looking at what you have, and leaves you notes you can maintain without them. Six questions in the first call will tell you which kind of developer you're talking to.

I'm a GoHighLevel developer, so weigh this accordingly. It's the checklist I'd want a client to run on me, built from the accounts I've been asked to fix after someone else built them.

## What does a GoHighLevel developer actually do?

They design how leads move through your business and build it in GoHighLevel: forms and calendars that create contacts, pipelines that match how you actually sell, workflows that assign owners and send follow-ups, custom fields that hold the facts you report on, and connections to the other tools you use, through webhooks, the API, or a tool like n8n.

That's different from a virtual assistant who sets up what you've already designed, and from a funnel designer who builds pages. All three are useful. The mistake is hiring one when you need another. If you know exactly what you want and someone just needs to click it into place, a VA is cheaper. If leads come from several places, more than one person follows up, or GoHighLevel has to talk to Shopify, Stripe, or your booking system, you need someone who designs the logic.

## Do you need one at all?

Not always. Set it up yourself if you have one pipeline, one person following up, and a couple of simple automations like a booking confirmation and a reminder. GoHighLevel's own templates and snapshots cover a lot of that.

Hire when one of these is true:

- **Leads arrive from several sources** and need routing to the right person.
- **Follow-up depends on what the lead does**, like replying, booking, paying, or going quiet.
- **GoHighLevel has to exchange data with another system**, in either direction.
- **The account is already messy**, with duplicate fields, half-finished workflows, and reports nobody trusts.

If it's the last one, ask for an audit before a build. Building on top of a messy account just makes a bigger mess.

Why each one matters:

- **Whose account?** The build should live in your sub-account, with the developer added as a user you can remove. Work built in someone else's account is theirs until it's moved, and moving it is a job of its own.
- **How do you test?** Workflows fail quietly. A developer who runs real test contacts through every branch, and shows you what happened, catches the broken step before your leads find it.
- **What stops a follow-up?** Every sequence needs an exit: a goal like a booking or a payment, or a stop when the lead replies. Without one, people who already bought keep getting "still interested?" texts.
- **How do you price it?** A fixed quote after a scoping call or an audit means they've looked at your account and committed to an outcome. Hourly is fine for small fixes. "We'll figure it out" isn't a price.
- **What do I get at the end?** Working workflows aren't enough. You need to know what each one does, what starts it, and what stops it, or every change means calling the developer back.
- **When would you say no?** GoHighLevel can't do everything well. A developer who says so, and suggests an alternative, is one who won't bend your account into a shape it can't hold.

## What are the red flags?

- **Asking for your password.** They should have their own user with the permissions the job needs. Shared logins mean you can't see who changed what, or take access back.
- **Quoting before asking how you sell.** A price without questions is a price for a template.
- **Tools in their name.** If the build depends on an n8n, Zapier, or Make account, it should be yours, with credentials you hold.
- **No test plan.** "It looks right in the builder" isn't testing.
- **"Anything is possible."** It isn't, and the things that aren't will show up after you've paid.

## How should the first project be structured?

Start with one small, paid, well-defined piece, and write down what "done" means before work starts. A first project might look like this:

| Piece | Done when |
|---|---|
| Lead intake | Every form and ad lead becomes one contact, tagged with its source |
| Routing | Every new lead has an owner within a minute of arriving |
| Follow-up | Texts and emails stop the moment the lead replies or books |
| Pipeline | Every open deal has a stage, a value, and a next task |
| Handover | Each workflow has a short note, and you have a recorded walkthrough |

Each line is something you can check yourself. If the first project goes well, the second one is easier to scope, because you both know how the other works.

## What should you have at the end?

- **Everything in your account**, owned by you.
- **A list of workflows** with what starts each one and what stops it.
- **Custom fields with one purpose each**, and no duplicates.
- **Access you can revoke**, by removing the developer's user.
- **Any outside tools in your name**, with the logins and API keys stored where you can find them.

If you have all five, you can hire someone else next time, or change it yourself. That's the real test of a good build.

## Key takeaways

- Hire a developer when logic crosses people, sources, or systems; a VA when the design is already done.
- Ask the six questions before you talk about price.
- The build lives in your account, under the developer's own removable user.
- Start with a small, paid project with written "done when" checks.
- Finish with notes, a walkthrough, and every outside tool in your name.

## FAQ

### How much does a GoHighLevel developer cost?

It varies widely with experience and scope. Small fixes are usually hourly; full builds are usually quoted as fixed projects after a scoping call or an audit. Compare quotes on what's included, especially testing and handover, not just the number.

### Should I give a GoHighLevel developer my login?

No. Add them as a user in your sub-account with only the permissions the job needs, and remove that user when the project ends.

### What's the difference between a GoHighLevel VA and a developer?

A VA sets up what you've already designed. A developer designs how leads move through the account, builds the logic, and connects GoHighLevel to other systems. Many projects need both at different times.

### Can a GoHighLevel developer connect it to other tools?

Yes, through webhooks, GoHighLevel's API, or an automation tool like n8n, Zapier, or Make. Make sure any outside tool account is in your name.

### Should I start with an audit or a build?

Start with an audit if the account already has workflows, fields, and pipelines that nobody fully understands. Start with a build if the account is new or nearly empty.
